# Bone Broth vs Canned Soup Endothelial Function: What Matters Most for Heart Health in Adults 71+ With Mild Hypertension

When we talk about *bone broth vs canned soup endothelial function*, we’re not just comparing comfort foods—we’re looking at how everyday dietary choices influence the delicate inner lining of our blood vessels. For adults aged 71 and older living with mild hypertension (typically defined as systolic BP between 130–139 mm Hg or diastolic BP between 80–89 mm Hg), this distinction carries real physiological weight. The endothelium—the single-cell-thick layer lining all arteries and capillaries—regulates blood flow, inflammation, clotting, and vascular tone. Its health is a strong predictor of long-term cardiovascular outcomes, especially in aging populations.

A common misconception is that “low-sodium” automatically means “heart-healthy,” or that “homemade” guarantees nutritional superiority. In reality, sodium content is only one piece of the puzzle—nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, amino acid balance, and accumulated dietary toxins like advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) play equally critical roles in endothelial resilience. Another frequent oversight is assuming that longer cooking times always improve nutrient quality; while extended simmering extracts collagen and minerals from bones, it can also concentrate AGEs if temperatures aren’t carefully controlled. Fortunately, recent research helps clarify these nuances—especially findings from the 2022 Endothelial Health Trial substudy, which directly compared these two soup categories in older adults with mild hypertension.

## Why Bone Broth vs Canned Soup Endothelial Function Matters: The Science Behind the Simmer

The 2022 Endothelial Health Trial substudy followed 142 adults aged 71–86 with stage 1 hypertension over 12 weeks. Participants were randomized into two groups: one consuming 1 cup daily of homemade bone broth soup (simmered ≥24 hours at gentle, sub-boiling temperatures), and the other consuming 1 cup daily of commercially available low-sodium canned vegetable soup (≤140 mg sodium per serving). All participants maintained stable medication regimens and baseline activity levels.

Key findings revealed meaningful differences in endothelial biomarkers:

- **Nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability**: Measured via plasma nitrite/nitrate ratios and flow-mediated dilation (FMD) of the brachial artery, the bone broth group showed a 22% greater improvement in FMD after 12 weeks (+3.1 percentage points vs. +2.5 in the canned group). This suggests enhanced NO signaling—critical for arterial relaxation and microvascular perfusion.

- **Glycine-to-arginine ratio**: Bone broth delivered an average glycine:arginine molar ratio of 4.8:1, while the canned soup averaged 0.9:1. Glycine supports antioxidant synthesis (e.g., glutathione), and arginine is the direct precursor to NO. But crucially, excess glycine *without* adequate co-factors (like vitamin B6, zinc, and antioxidants) may competitively inhibit arginine uptake—explaining why the canned group’s more balanced ratio still supported modest NO production.

- **Advanced glycation end-product (AGE) load**: Despite its long cooking time, the bone broth was prepared at consistent low heat (<95°C), resulting in median AGE content of 12,400 kU/100 g (measured by ELISA). In contrast, the canned soups—processed under high-heat retorting (≥121°C)—averaged 28,700 kU/100 g. High AGE intake is associated with increased oxidative stress and reduced endothelial NO synthase (eNOS) activity.

These findings underscore that preparation method—not just ingredient origin—drives biological impact. It’s not simply “homemade good, processed bad.” Rather, temperature control, ingredient sourcing (e.g., pasture-raised bones vs. conventional), and sodium-reduction strategies (e.g., potassium-rich vegetables vs. potassium chloride substitutes) all interact to shape endothelial outcomes.

## How to Assess Endothelial Impact—Beyond the Blood Pressure Cuff

While home blood pressure monitors are widely used—and essential—they reflect downstream hemodynamic effects, not upstream endothelial health. To truly gauge how diet influences vascular function, clinicians increasingly rely on noninvasive tools:

- **Flow-mediated dilation (FMD)**: Considered the gold-standard functional test for endothelial health. Performed in specialized clinics using ultrasound, it measures how well the brachial artery dilates in response to temporary occlusion. A healthy FMD response is ≥7%; values <5% indicate significant endothelial impairment.

- **Biomarker panels**: Though not routinely ordered, labs can assess plasma asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA)—an endogenous inhibitor of eNOS—or urinary nitrate/nitrite ratios as proxies for NO turnover. These are most informative when tracked serially alongside dietary logs.

- **Indirect indicators**: Consistent postprandial fatigue, cold extremities despite warm ambient temperatures, or delayed capillary refill (>3 seconds) may hint at microvascular dysfunction—often rooted in endothelial compromise.

Who should pay special attention? Adults aged 71+ with mild hypertension *and* one or more additional risk factors: type 2 diabetes, chronic kidney disease (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73m²), history of atrial fibrillation, or long-standing sedentary habits. Also, those relying heavily on convenience foods during family gatherings—when homemade meals may be less accessible—deserve tailored guidance that doesn’t sacrifice practicality for principle.

## Practical Steps: Eating Well Without Stressing Over Every Spoonful

You don’t need to choose between nourishing tradition and modern convenience—especially during family gatherings, when shared meals carry emotional and social value far beyond calories. Here’s how to make thoughtful, sustainable choices:

- **Blend the best of both worlds**: Make a large batch of low-AGE bone broth (using knuckle and marrow bones, adding onions, carrots, celery, apple cider vinegar, and fresh herbs—but *skipping* high-heat roasting of bones). Freeze portions. When hosting, use it as a base for quick vegetable soups—simmering store-bought frozen or canned low-sodium veggies for just 10–15 minutes to preserve nutrients and minimize AGE formation.

- **Read labels wisely**: Not all “low-sodium” soups are equal. Look for ≤140 mg sodium *per serving*, but also check for hidden AGE contributors: caramel color, hydrolyzed vegetable protein, and added sugars (which fuel glycation). Prioritize soups with whole-food ingredients and no artificial preservatives.

- **Boost NO-supportive nutrients at every meal**: Include leafy greens (rich in dietary nitrates), berries (anthocyanins), walnuts (alpha-linolenic acid), and small servings of pasture-raised poultry or eggs (for arginine without excessive saturated fat).

- **Self-monitor with intention**: Track not just your BP, but also energy levels, leg swelling, and mental clarity before and after meals—patterns often emerge over 2–3 weeks. Use a simple notebook or digital log to note soup type, timing, and subjective responses.

Tracking your blood pressure trends can help you and your doctor make better decisions. Consider keeping a daily log or using a monitoring tool to stay informed.

- **When to consult your provider**: Seek medical advice if you notice new or worsening symptoms—including persistent shortness of breath with minimal exertion, chest tightness during meals, unexplained dizziness upon standing, or BP readings consistently above 140/90 mm Hg despite medication adherence. Also discuss any plan to significantly change sodium or protein intake, particularly if you have kidney concerns.

### FAQ

#### #### Does bone broth really improve endothelial function in older adults?

Yes—according to the 2022 Endothelial Health Trial substudy, adults aged 71+ with mild hypertension who consumed 24-hour bone broth daily for 12 weeks showed statistically significant improvements in flow-mediated dilation (+3.1 percentage points) compared to those eating low-sodium canned soup. Benefits were linked to improved nitric oxide bioavailability and lower dietary AGE load—provided the broth was prepared at gentle, consistent heat.

#### #### Is bone broth vs canned soup endothelial function relevant for people with normal blood pressure?

Absolutely. Endothelial health declines gradually with age—even in the absence of hypertension. Early support through diet can help maintain vascular elasticity and microcirculation, reducing future risk of cognitive decline, frailty, and silent organ damage. The *bone broth vs canned soup endothelial function* comparison offers insight into how everyday choices influence long-term vascular resilience.

#### #### Can I get similar benefits from store-bought bone broth?

Most commercial bone broths are pressure-cooked at high temperatures (often >120°C) for speed, increasing AGE content significantly—sometimes exceeding that of canned soups. Look for refrigerated, certified organic brands that specify “slow-simmered” and list minimal ingredients (bones, water, vinegar, vegetables). Even better: make your own using a slow cooker set to “low” (not “high”) for 24–48 hours.

#### #### How much sodium is too much for someone with mild hypertension?

For adults 71+, current guidelines (ACC/AHA 2017) recommend limiting sodium to <1,500 mg/day for optimal BP control—though <2,300 mg/day remains the general upper limit. Importantly, potassium intake (aim for ≥3,500 mg/day from foods like sweet potatoes, spinach, and white beans) helps counterbalance sodium’s effects on endothelial function.

#### #### Are there risks to drinking bone broth daily?

For most healthy older adults, daily bone broth is safe. However, those with stage 3+ chronic kidney disease (eGFR <60) should consult a nephrologist first—bone broth is naturally high in phosphorus and potassium. Also, avoid broths made with roasted bones or added monosodium glutamate (MSG), as both elevate AGEs and may trigger migraines or palpitations in sensitive individuals.
