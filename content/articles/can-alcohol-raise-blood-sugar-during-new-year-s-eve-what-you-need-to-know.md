# How Alcohol and Blood Sugar Holidays Interact—What Older Adults with Diabetes Should Know

If you’re over 50 and living with diabetes, the phrase *alcohol and blood sugar holidays* might sound like a gentle reminder—not a warning. During festive seasons like Christmas and New Year’s Eve, it’s natural to want to relax, share a toast, and enjoy time with loved ones. But for many older adults, those celebratory sips can quietly influence glucose levels in ways that aren’t always obvious—especially when routines shift, meals are irregular, or stress and sleep patterns change. Understanding how alcohol interacts with your body’s ability to manage blood sugar helps you stay safe *and* joyful.

It’s common to assume “a little wine won’t hurt” or that “only sugary drinks raise blood sugar”—but both ideas miss important nuances. Alcohol doesn’t contain carbohydrates like soda or juice, yet it can still cause unexpected lows (hypoglycemia) or, in some cases, contribute to elevated readings—particularly when paired with certain foods, medications, or lifestyle changes common during the holidays. The good news? With a few mindful choices, you can fully participate in the season while keeping your health steady.

## Why Alcohol and Blood Sugar Holidays Matter for Your Body

Alcohol affects blood sugar in two primary, sometimes opposing, ways—and which effect dominates depends on several factors, including what and how much you drink, whether you’ve eaten, and your current diabetes management plan.

First, alcohol *inhibits the liver’s ability to produce glucose*. Normally, your liver releases stored glucose (glycogen) between meals or overnight to keep blood sugar stable. But when alcohol is present, the liver prioritizes metabolizing it—putting glucose production on hold. This increases the risk of hypoglycemia, especially if you’re taking insulin or insulin-stimulating medications like sulfonylureas (e.g., glipizide or glyburide). For older adults, this risk is heightened due to slower metabolism, reduced kidney function, and often less noticeable symptoms of low blood sugar—like confusion or dizziness being mistaken for fatigue or holiday stress.

Second, many holiday drinks *do* contain added sugars or carbs: eggnog (up to 20 g of sugar per cup), mulled wine, sweetened cocktails, and even some craft beers. These can cause post-drink spikes—particularly if consumed without food. Mixed with the delayed liver response, this creates a “rollercoaster” effect: an initial rise followed by a potentially dangerous drop hours later—even overnight.

It’s also worth noting that alcohol can mask symptoms of both high and low blood sugar. A flushed face or slurred speech may be chalked up to celebration rather than hypoglycemia; fatigue or irritability could be dismissed as holiday burnout instead of hyperglycemia. For adults aged 50 and up, whose bodies process substances more slowly and whose hormonal responses may be less robust, these overlaps matter more than ever.

## Who Should Pay Special Attention During the Festive Season?

While anyone with diabetes benefits from thoughtful drinking habits, certain groups should take extra care:

- **People using insulin or sulfonylureas**: These medications carry the highest risk of alcohol-related hypoglycemia—especially if doses aren’t adjusted around drinking or meals are skipped.
- **Those with neuropathy or kidney disease**: Reduced sensation may make it harder to detect early signs of low blood sugar, and impaired kidney function affects how long medications—and alcohol—stay in your system.
- **Adults managing both diabetes and hypertension**: Alcohol can temporarily raise blood pressure (BP), and repeated heavy use may contribute to sustained elevations. Since about 70% of adults over 65 have high BP, this dual impact deserves attention—especially when combined with salty party snacks or disrupted sleep.
- **Individuals with a history of hypoglycemia unawareness**: If you’ve had episodes where blood sugar dropped without typical warning signs (shakiness, sweating, hunger), alcohol significantly increases recurrence risk.

Also, consider timing: New Year’s Eve often means late nights, delayed dinners, and spontaneous toasts. These shifts disrupt your usual rhythm—making glucose control less predictable. That’s why awareness, preparation, and consistency matter more than restriction.

## Practical Tips for Safer Celebrations

You don’t need to skip the toast—or the joy—to stay healthy. Here’s how to navigate *alcohol and blood sugar holidays* thoughtfully:

✅ **Choose wisely**: Opt for lower-carb, lower-sugar options. Dry wines (e.g., pinot noir or sauvignon blanc), light beer (under 100 calories), or spirits like vodka or gin mixed with soda water and a squeeze of lime contain minimal carbs—typically under 1–2 g per serving. Avoid pre-mixed cocktails, dessert wines, and liqueurs, which can pack 15–30 g of sugar per serving.

✅ **Always pair with food**: Never drink on an empty stomach. Eat a balanced snack or meal containing protein, healthy fat, and complex carbs before and while drinking. Think grilled salmon with roasted vegetables, hummus and whole-grain crackers, or turkey roll-ups with avocado.

✅ **Space it out**: Stick to recommended limits—no more than one drink per day for women and two for men—but remember: “one drink” means 5 oz of wine, 12 oz of beer, or 1.5 oz of distilled spirits. Consider alternating alcoholic drinks with sparkling water or herbal tea to stay hydrated and slow intake.

✅ **Check before and after**: Test your blood sugar before drinking, 2 hours after your first drink, and again before bed—especially if you’ve had more than one drink or plan to sleep soon. Keep fast-acting glucose (like glucose tablets or juice) nearby, just in case.

✅ **Communicate with your care team**: Let your doctor or diabetes educator know about your holiday plans. They may suggest temporary adjustments to medication timing or dosing—especially if you’ll be fasting for part of the day or eating at unusual hours.

Tracking your blood pressure trends can help you and your doctor make better decisions. Consider keeping a daily log or using a monitoring tool to stay informed.

⚠️ **When to see a doctor**: Contact your healthcare provider if you experience frequent lows (below 70 mg/dL) despite precautions, unexplained highs above 250 mg/dL lasting more than a day, confusion or difficulty waking after drinking, or persistent nausea/vomiting. Also seek guidance if you notice new or worsening dizziness, chest discomfort, or shortness of breath—these could signal interactions between alcohol, medications, and cardiovascular health.

## A Reassuring Note for the Season Ahead

The holidays are about connection, comfort, and kindness—including kindness toward yourself. You don’t have to choose between celebration and care. With a little planning and awareness, you can enjoy festive moments without compromising your well-being. Remember: small, consistent choices add up. Whether it’s choosing sparkling water with a twist of orange, sharing stories instead of shots, or simply pausing to check in with your body—you’re honoring both your health and your joy. If you're unsure, talking to your doctor is always a good idea. And yes—*alcohol and blood sugar holidays* can coexist peacefully, as long as you’re informed, prepared, and gentle with yourself.

### FAQ

#### #### Does alcohol raise blood sugar during Christmas and New Year?
Not directly—but it can indirectly lead to higher readings when mixed with sugary drinks or rich foods. More commonly, alcohol lowers blood sugar by interfering with liver glucose release—especially in people using insulin or certain diabetes medications. The net effect depends on what you drink, how much, and whether you’ve eaten.

#### #### How does alcohol affect blood sugar in older adults during the holidays?
Older adults metabolize alcohol more slowly and may have reduced liver and kidney function, making them more sensitive to its effects on glucose regulation. Combined with irregular meals, less physical activity, and possible medication changes during travel or gatherings, this increases the risk of both hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia—making careful monitoring especially important during *alcohol and blood sugar holidays*.

#### #### Can I drink alcohol safely if I’m managing blood sugar during Christmas and New Year?
Yes—many people with diabetes do so safely by choosing low-sugar options, eating before and while drinking, limiting portions, and checking blood sugar regularly. It helps to discuss your plans with your doctor ahead of time, especially if you take insulin or other glucose-lowering medications.

#### #### What are the best low-sugar holiday drinks for people watching their blood sugar?
Great choices include dry red or white wine (4–5 g carbs per 5 oz), light beer (5–10 g carbs per 12 oz), or spirits (vodka, gin, rum) mixed with unsweetened sparkling water and fresh citrus. Avoid eggnog, cider, margaritas, and most premade cocktails—they often contain 20–40 g of added sugar per serving.

#### #### Does alcohol raise blood pressure during New Year’s Eve?
Yes—alcohol can cause a short-term increase in blood pressure, especially with binge drinking (defined as 4+ drinks for women or 5+ for men in about 2 hours). Even moderate amounts may elevate BP temporarily. For adults over 50 managing both hypertension and diabetes, pairing alcohol with salty snacks or late-night stress can amplify this effect—so pacing and hydration matter.
