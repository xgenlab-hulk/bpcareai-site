# When to Consider Pheochromocytoma in Adults 57–70 With Episodic Palpitations, Normal Echo, and Normal Urinary Metanephrines—but Elevated Plasma Free Metanephrines

If you’re in your late 50s or 60s and experiencing sudden, unpredictable palpitations—especially with spikes in blood pressure (BP)—but your standard tests come back reassuring, it’s natural to feel puzzled. That’s where *pheochromocytoma suspicion adults 57-70 normal urinary metanephrines* becomes an important, though often overlooked, consideration. While pheochromocytoma is rare (affecting only about 2–8 per million adults annually), it’s one of the few treatable causes of secondary hypertension—and catching it early makes all the difference. A common misconception is that “normal 24-hour urine metanephrines” rule out the condition entirely. In reality, up to 15% of pheochromocytomas—particularly smaller, intermittent, or SDHB-related tumors—may show normal urinary metabolites but elevated plasma free metanephrines. Another myth is that age alone protects you: while incidence peaks around 40–50, diagnoses in adults 57–70 are not uncommon—and often delayed due to attribution of symptoms to stress, menopause, or aging.

## Why Pheochromocytoma Suspicion Adults 57–70 Matters

Pheochromocytomas are rare, usually benign adrenal tumors that secrete catecholamines—norepinephrine and epinephrine—which drive episodic surges in heart rate and arterial pressure. In adults aged 57–70, symptoms like palpitations, sweating, headache, and anxiety may be mistaken for anxiety disorders, cardiac arrhythmias, or even early heart failure—especially when echocardiograms (echo) appear normal and routine BP checks fall within “acceptable” ranges between episodes. What makes this age group particularly nuanced is that catecholamine secretion can be highly variable: some tumors release bursts only under certain physiological stresses (e.g., posture changes, fasting, or even lab draw), leading to false-negative urine tests. Plasma free metanephrines, collected after proper patient preparation (supine rest, no caffeine or MAO inhibitors for 48 hours), are more sensitive—detecting ~96% of tumors—even when urinary values sit just below cutoffs.

## How to Confirm—or Rule Out—Pheochromocytoma Thoughtfully

When plasma free metanephrines are elevated but urinary metanephrines are normal, the next step isn’t immediate imaging—it’s confirmation. Clonidine suppression testing helps distinguish true tumor activity from “false-positive” elevations caused by stress, renal impairment, or medications (e.g., tricyclics, SNRIs). In this test, clonidine (an alpha-2 agonist) is given orally; in healthy individuals, it suppresses catecholamine release and lowers plasma metanephrines by ≥40% within 3 hours. Failure to suppress supports pheochromocytoma. Importantly, genetic screening for *SDHB* and *SDHD* mutations should be offered—even without family history—as these variants are linked to later-onset, extra-adrenal, and more aggressive tumors. Imaging timing also matters: avoid CT/MRI during acute symptom flares (risk of contrast-induced crisis) and wait until BP is stable (ideally <140/90 mm Hg on treatment). Delayed imaging (e.g., 2–4 weeks post-stabilization) reduces false negatives from transient adrenal hyperplasia or incidental nodules.

## Practical Steps You Can Take Right Now

You don’t need to wait for your next appointment to support your care journey. First, minimize known catecholamine triggers: limit caffeine, avoid tyramine-rich foods (aged cheeses, fermented meats), and practice gentle breathing techniques during palpitations—this helps reduce sympathetic surges. Second, monitor your BP at home—not just once a day, but at consistent times (e.g., morning upon waking, midday, and evening), and especially *during* or immediately after an episode if safe to do so. Note posture (seated vs. standing), recent meals, and emotional state alongside each reading. Avoid checking BP right after exercise or strong emotions, as those reflect expected short-term rises—not pathology. Tracking your blood pressure trends can help you and your doctor make better decisions. Consider keeping a daily log or using a monitoring tool to stay informed. Finally, contact your provider promptly if you notice three or more of the following together: systolic BP >180 mm Hg *with* palpitations, unexplained weight loss, persistent tremor, or orthostatic dizziness—especially if episodes occur more than twice weekly.

It’s completely understandable to feel uncertain when test results seem contradictory. But remember: modern diagnostics are designed to clarify—not confuse—and your awareness and advocacy are powerful tools. If you're unsure, talking to your doctor is always a good idea. With thoughtful follow-up—including clonidine testing, genetic evaluation, and well-timed imaging—the path to clarity is both clear and compassionate. ### FAQ

#### #### What does it mean if my urinary metanephrines are normal but plasma free metanephrines are high?
It suggests possible intermittent or low-volume catecholamine secretion—common in smaller pheochromocytomas or those linked to *SDHB* mutations. Plasma testing is more sensitive, especially in older adults, and warrants further evaluation—not dismissal.

#### #### Can pheochromocytoma suspicion adults 57-70 normal urinary metanephrines be missed on initial testing?
Yes—up to 15% of cases show normal urinary metanephrines. This is why guidelines now recommend plasma free metanephrines as the first-line biochemical test for adults over 50 with suggestive symptoms and normal echo.

#### #### Why would my doctor order genetic testing if I’m 62 and have no family history of pheochromocytoma?
About 30–40% of pheochromocytomas in adults over 50 are associated with hereditary syndromes—even without obvious family patterns. *SDHB* and *SDHD* variants, in particular, can present later in life and carry higher risks for malignancy, making early identification clinically meaningful.

#### #### Is clonidine suppression testing safe for someone in their 60s?
Yes—when done under supervision and with BP monitored closely before and after dosing. It’s well tolerated in older adults and remains the gold-standard functional test to confirm autonomous catecholamine production.

#### #### How long should I wait after a BP spike to get tested for pheochromocytoma?
Ideally, blood draws for plasma metanephrines should happen after at least 30 minutes of quiet supine rest—regardless of recent symptoms. Timing matters less than proper preparation: avoid caffeine, nicotine, and certain meds for 48 hours beforehand.
