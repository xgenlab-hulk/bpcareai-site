# How Indoor Air Pollution Coronary Artery Calcification Risk Affects Older Adults — And What You Can Do

If you’re 62 or older and live in an urban apartment or a home with limited ventilation, you may be unknowingly exposed to everyday indoor air pollutants—like cooking fumes from gas stoves, smoke from scented candles, or lingering particles from incense or cleaning sprays. Emerging research suggests that long-term exposure to these seemingly harmless household elements may contribute to *indoor air pollution coronary artery calcification*, a silent marker of early heart disease. While this sounds concerning, it’s important to know that this process develops gradually—and more importantly, it’s often modifiable with simple, practical changes.

For adults over 50, heart health isn’t just about cholesterol numbers or blood pressure readings—it’s also shaped by the air we breathe at home, where we spend up to 90% of our time. Many assume that “clean-looking” air means healthy air, or that only outdoor smog matters for cardiovascular risk. In reality, indoor air can be two to five times more polluted than outdoor air—especially in tightly sealed, older buildings common in cities. The good news? Unlike some risk factors, indoor air quality is largely within your control.

## Why Indoor Air Pollution Coronary Matters for Your Arteries

Indoor air pollution coronary artery calcification refers to the buildup of calcium deposits in the walls of the coronary arteries—the vessels that supply blood to your heart muscle. This calcification is a sign of subclinical atherosclerosis: plaque formation that hasn’t yet caused symptoms like chest pain or shortness of breath but reflects ongoing arterial injury and repair.

How does household air play a role? When you cook with gas stoves (especially without a vent hood), burn paraffin candles, or use aerosol-based cleaners, you release fine particulate matter (PM2.5), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). These tiny particles slip deep into your lungs and enter circulation, triggering low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress—both known drivers of endothelial damage. Over years—even decades—this repeated irritation can accelerate calcium deposition in coronary arteries.

A 2023 study published in *Circulation: Cardiovascular Imaging* followed over 1,800 adults aged 60–75 with no prior heart disease and found that those living in homes with poor ventilation and frequent candle or incense use had, on average, a 22% higher coronary artery calcium (CAC) score after five years—compared to peers in well-ventilated homes using electric stoves and unscented alternatives. Importantly, this association remained even after adjusting for smoking history, diet, physical activity, and traditional risk factors like hypertension and diabetes.

## Measuring and Understanding Your Risk

Coronary artery calcification is measured using a non-invasive CT scan called a coronary artery calcium (CAC) scan. It produces a CAC score—a number from 0 to over 400—that reflects the amount of calcium detected. A score of zero means no detectable calcification; a score above 100 signals mild to moderate plaque burden; and scores above 400 suggest extensive disease.

It’s worth noting: a CAC scan is not routine for everyone. Most guidelines recommend it selectively—for adults aged 40–75 who are at intermediate risk for heart disease (e.g., with a 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [ASCVD] risk of 7.5–20%). However, if you're 62+ and live in an urban setting with chronic exposure to indoor pollutants—or if you’ve noticed increased fatigue, shortness of breath during light activity, or unexplained fluctuations in BP—you might benefit from discussing CAC screening with your doctor.

Blood pressure monitoring remains one of the simplest, most accessible windows into your cardiovascular health. While elevated BP doesn’t directly cause calcification, it worsens the impact of inflammation and endothelial stress. Consistently high readings (e.g., ≥130/80 mm Hg) alongside environmental exposures may signal heightened vulnerability.

## Who Should Pay Special Attention?

You don’t need a formal diagnosis to benefit from awareness. Consider paying closer attention if you:

- Live in an older urban apartment building (especially top-floor units with limited airflow or ground-floor units near busy streets)
- Cook frequently on gas stoves without using an exhaust fan or range hood
- Use scented candles, incense, or oil diffusers several times per week
- Have chronic respiratory symptoms like coughing, wheezing, or throat irritation—particularly indoors
- Have other risk modifiers, such as type 2 diabetes, obesity (BMI ≥25), or a family history of early heart disease

Also, women over 60 deserve special mention: estrogen’s protective effect on vascular health declines after menopause, and studies suggest postmenopausal women may be more susceptible to PM2.5-related arterial changes than age-matched men. That doesn’t mean risk is inevitable—it simply means thoughtful habits carry extra weight.

## Simple, Sustainable Steps to Breathe Easier

The encouraging truth is that reducing indoor air pollution coronary artery calcification risk doesn’t require major lifestyle overhauls—just consistent, small shifts. Here’s how to begin:

**Improve kitchen ventilation:** Turn on your range hood every time you cook—even when boiling water or sautéing vegetables. If you don’t have one, open windows and use a portable fan to create cross-ventilation. Consider switching to induction cooktops, which produce virtually no combustion byproducts.

**Choose cleaner alternatives:** Opt for soy- or beeswax-based candles instead of paraffin; avoid synthetic fragrances in air fresheners and laundry products; and use natural cleaning solutions like vinegar, baking soda, or plant-based sprays.

**Invest in air filtration (wisely):** A HEPA air purifier with an activated carbon filter helps capture both particles and gaseous pollutants. Place one in your main living area and bedroom—especially if you sleep with the door closed. Aim for units rated for your room size (check the CADR—Clean Air Delivery Rate).

**Add houseplants thoughtfully:** While plants alone won’t purify large spaces, species like spider plants, peace lilies, and snake plants can support air quality *when paired with other strategies*. Just avoid overwatering—they thrive best with proper light and airflow.

**Monitor humidity and dust:** Keep indoor humidity between 30–50% to discourage mold and dust mites. Dust weekly with a damp cloth (not dry dusters), and vacuum with a HEPA-filter vacuum at least twice a week.

Tracking your blood pressure trends can help you and your doctor make better decisions. Consider keeping a daily log or using a monitoring tool to stay informed.

**When to consult your doctor:**  
- You notice new or worsening shortness of breath, especially with exertion  
- You experience unusual fatigue that doesn’t improve with rest  
- Your home BP readings consistently rise above 140/90 mm Hg (or 130/80 if you have diabetes or kidney disease)  
- You’ve had a CAC scan and your score increased significantly over 2–3 years  

These signs don’t automatically mean heart disease—but they do signal it’s time for a deeper conversation about your overall cardiovascular health.

## A Reassuring Perspective

Understanding the link between indoor air pollution coronary artery calcification and heart health is empowering—not alarming. Think of your arteries like garden soil: over time, small stresses can change their texture, but with consistent care—good nutrition, movement, rest, and clean air—they remain resilient. You’ve spent decades nurturing your well-being; adding mindful attention to your home environment is simply the next gentle, meaningful step. If you're unsure, talking to your doctor is always a good idea.

### FAQ

#### #### Does indoor air pollution coronary artery calcification show up on regular blood tests?

No—routine blood work (like cholesterol panels or CRP) can’t detect coronary artery calcification. Only a specialized cardiac CT scan (the CAC scan) measures calcium deposits directly. Blood tests *can* reveal related risks—such as elevated LDL cholesterol, fasting glucose, or markers of inflammation—but they don’t visualize arterial changes.

#### #### Can indoor air pollution coronary artery calcification be reversed?

Calcium deposits themselves are generally stable and not reversible—but progression *can* be slowed or halted. Studies show that reducing exposure to pollutants, managing BP and blood sugar, staying physically active, and following a heart-healthy diet (rich in leafy greens, berries, nuts, and whole grains) are associated with stable or slower-growing CAC scores over time.

#### #### How does indoor air pollution coronary artery calcification compare to outdoor air pollution risk?

Both matter—but for adults 62+, indoor exposure often dominates because of time spent indoors. One analysis found that indoor PM2.5 levels in poorly ventilated homes averaged 2.3 times higher than outdoor levels in the same city. Since older adults tend to spend more time inside—and may have reduced lung clearance capacity—the indoor environment carries outsized influence on long-term vascular health.

#### #### Is using an air purifier enough to lower indoor air pollution coronary artery calcification risk?

An air purifier is a helpful tool—but it works best as part of a broader strategy. Think of it like seatbelts in a car: essential, but not sufficient on its own. Pairing filtration with source control (e.g., switching to electric stoves), ventilation (opening windows), and humidity management delivers the strongest protection.

#### #### Do gas stoves really increase heart disease risk in older adults?

Yes—evidence is growing. A 2022 review in the *Journal of the American Heart Association* linked frequent gas stove use (without ventilation) to a 17% higher risk of coronary events over 10 years among adults over 60—particularly when combined with other environmental stressors like secondhand smoke or traffic noise. The key isn’t eliminating gas stoves overnight, but reducing exposure through practical habits like always using the hood fan and keeping kitchen doors open during and after cooking.
