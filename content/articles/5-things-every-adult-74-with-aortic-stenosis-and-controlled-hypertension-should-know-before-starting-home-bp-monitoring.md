# Why Do Wrist BP Monitors Fail Adults 74+ With Aortic Stenosis? (5 Critical Monitoring Errors)

If you're 74 or older and living with both aortic stenosis and well-managed hypertension, starting home blood pressure monitoring can be a valuable part of your care—but it comes with important nuances. Unlike younger adults or those without valvular disease, your arterial pressure readings may not tell the full story due to changes in heart function, vessel stiffness, and how blood flows through a narrowed aortic valve. Many assume that "normal" BP numbers (like <130/80 mm Hg) apply universally—but for people with fixed-outflow obstruction, overly aggressive lowering can reduce coronary perfusion and increase fall risk. Others mistakenly believe wrist or finger devices are just as reliable as upper-arm cuffs—yet these often fail in older adults with stiff arteries or low stroke volume.

Understanding the unique physiology behind your readings helps avoid unnecessary worry—or worse, inappropriate treatment changes. Let's break down five key considerations grounded in current cardiology guidance.

## Why aortic stenosis home blood pressure monitoring requires special attention

Aortic stenosis creates a fixed obstruction: the left ventricle must pump harder to push blood through a narrowed valve. Over time, this leads to left ventricular hypertrophy, reduced arterial compliance, and altered central-to-peripheral pressure wave transmission. As a result, radial (wrist) waveforms frequently show *augmentation*—meaning systolic pressure appears artificially high and pulse pressure widened—due to wave reflection off stiff peripheral arteries. This is especially pronounced in older adults, where age-related arterial stiffening compounds the effect. In one study, radial-cuff measurements overestimated central systolic pressure by up to 25 mm Hg in patients with moderate-to-severe stenosis.

Additionally, many adults 74+ have sarcopenic obesity—a combination of muscle loss and increased adipose tissue—making standard cuff sizing unreliable. Using an undersized cuff inflates readings by 10–20 mm Hg; an oversized cuff may under-read by 5–10 mm Hg. Always confirm cuff fit: the bladder should encircle ≥80% of the arm circumference, with the lower edge 2–3 cm above the elbow crease.

## Interpreting pulse pressure—and why “wide” doesn’t always mean “uncontrolled”

In aortic stenosis, pulse pressure (the difference between systolic and diastolic BP) often widens—not because of improved elasticity, but due to reduced stroke volume and increased arterial stiffness. A pulse pressure >60 mm Hg is common, and >80 mm Hg may reflect advanced disease rather than isolated hypertension. Importantly, *narrowing* pulse pressure (<30 mm Hg) can signal worsening cardiac output and merits prompt evaluation.

For adults 74+ with controlled hypertension and stable stenosis, evidence-based targets prioritize safety over strict numbers:
- **Systolic BP goal**: 130–140 mm Hg (avoid <120 mm Hg unless exceptionally well-tolerated)
- **Pulse pressure goal**: ≤70 mm Hg (not a rigid cutoff, but a red flag if rising steadily over weeks)
- **Diastolic BP**: Maintain ≥60 mm Hg to preserve coronary filling—especially critical when aortic valve area is <1.0 cm²

Those with severe stenosis (aortic valve area <1.0 cm²), recent syncope, or NYHA Class II+ symptoms should discuss BP targets individually with their cardiologist before adjusting medications based on home readings.

## Practical steps for safe and accurate aortic stenosis home blood pressure monitoring

- Use an **upper-arm, automated, oscillometric device validated for arrhythmia and elderly populations** (e.g., meets ESH-IP or AAMI standards). Avoid wrist cuffs.
- Measure twice daily (morning before medications, evening before bed), seated quietly for 5 minutes, feet flat, arm supported at heart level.
- Record *both* systolic and diastolic values—and calculate pulse pressure each time (systolic minus diastolic).
- If readings consistently differ by >10 mm Hg between arms, use the higher-reading arm for all future checks—and mention this to your clinician.
- Avoid checking BP within 30 minutes of caffeine, meals, or walking—even light activity can elevate systolic pressure disproportionately in stenosis.

> **MEDICAL WARNING**
> Aortic stenosis changes how your body responds to blood pressure medications. **See your doctor immediately if you experience:**
> - Systolic BP drops below 110 mm Hg with dizziness or fatigue
> - Pulse pressure exceeds 80 mm Hg with new shortness of breath
> - Systolic BP rises >20 mm Hg above baseline for 3+ days
> - New chest pain, especially with exertion
> - Fainting or near-fainting episodes
>
> **Emergency warning (Call 911):** Loss of consciousness, severe chest pain at rest, sudden severe shortness of breath, rapid irregular heartbeat lasting >5 minutes, or signs of stroke. These may signal serious valve-related complications requiring immediate cardiac evaluation.

## A gentle closing note

Living well with aortic stenosis and hypertension is absolutely possible—and thoughtful home blood pressure monitoring supports that goal. Your readings are one piece of a larger clinical picture, not a verdict. Small, consistent habits—like proper cuff use, calm measurement conditions, and sharing trends with your care team—make a meaningful difference. If you're unsure, talking to your doctor is always a good idea.

### FAQ

#### Can I use a wrist blood pressure monitor if I have aortic stenosis?

No. Wrist monitors rely on radial artery waveforms, which are highly distorted in aortic stenosis due to altered pressure wave reflection and reduced stroke volume. Upper-arm cuffs provide more reliable systolic and pulse pressure data. The narrowed aortic valve creates abnormal pressure waves that travel differently through peripheral arteries, making wrist readings inaccurate by 15–25 mm Hg in many cases. Always use an upper-arm, oscillometric monitor validated for elderly populations and arrhythmias.

#### What's the safest systolic blood pressure target for adults 74+ with severe aortic stenosis?

For adults 74+ with severe aortic stenosis, a systolic target of 130–140 mm Hg is generally recommended. Going lower—especially below 120 mm Hg—may compromise coronary perfusion and increase syncope risk. The narrowed valve creates a fixed obstacle to blood flow, so blood pressure must be adequate to maintain perfusion to the heart muscle itself (which fills during diastole). Always individualize targets with your cardiologist based on your valve area, symptoms, and overall health status.

#### How does sarcopenic obesity affect home BP monitoring accuracy in older adults?

Sarcopenic obesity makes cuff sizing challenging: excess arm fat can mask underlying muscle loss, leading to routine use of standard cuffs that are too small. This causes falsely elevated readings—potentially prompting unnecessary antihypertensive intensification. Measuring arm circumference and selecting a large or extra-large cuff (per device guidelines) is essential. The bladder inside the cuff should encircle at least 80% of the arm circumference; if it doesn't, readings may be artificially high by 10–20 mm Hg.

#### Why does pulse pressure widen in aortic stenosis—and should I worry about it?

Widened pulse pressure reflects reduced stroke volume and increased arterial stiffness—not necessarily uncontrolled hypertension. While a pulse pressure >80 mm Hg warrants review, focus on trends over time and symptoms—not single numbers. It's a clue, not a crisis. What matters more is whether pulse pressure is *increasing* steadily over weeks or months, which could signal worsening valve function. Conversely, a *narrowing* pulse pressure (<30 mm Hg) may indicate reduced cardiac output and needs prompt medical evaluation.

#### Is home blood pressure monitoring useful before aortic valve replacement surgery?

Yes—especially in the months leading up to planned TAVR (transcatheter aortic valve replacement) or SAVR (surgical aortic valve replacement). Consistent home blood pressure monitoring helps establish your baseline, detect subtle decompensation, and guide perioperative BP management. Just ensure technique and device selection meet valvular-disease-monitoring standards: upper-arm cuff, proper sizing, consistent timing, and regular communication with your cardiology team about trends. Your surgical team will use this data to optimize your pre-operative condition and post-operative recovery plan.
