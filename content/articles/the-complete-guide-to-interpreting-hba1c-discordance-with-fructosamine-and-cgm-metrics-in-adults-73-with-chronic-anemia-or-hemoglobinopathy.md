# Understanding HbA1c Discordance With Fructosamine in Older Adults: A Practical Guide for Seniors With Anemia or Hemoglobinopathy

If you’re 73 or older and living with diabetes—or caring for someone who is—you may have noticed something puzzling: your HbA1c result doesn’t seem to match your daily glucose readings, your fructosamine level, or your continuous glucose monitor (CGM) data. This mismatch is known as *HbA1c discordance fructosamine seniors*, and it’s more common—and more clinically meaningful—than many realize. For adults over 70, especially those with chronic anemia or inherited hemoglobin variants (like sickle cell trait or thalassemia), standard HbA1c interpretation can be misleading. That’s because HbA1c reflects average blood glucose over ~3 months—but only if red blood cells live their full lifespan (~120 days). When anemia shortens red cell survival or hemoglobinopathies alter glycation kinetics, HbA1c becomes unreliable. A common misconception is that “a higher HbA1c always means worse glucose control”—but in seniors with altered hemoglobin turnover, it may simply reflect faster red cell turnover or abnormal hemoglobin structure. Another myth is that fructosamine is “just a backup test”; in reality, it offers complementary, often more accurate, insight when HbA1c is suspect.

## Why HbA1c Discordance Fructosamine Seniors Matters Clinically

HbA1c discordance fructosamine seniors arises primarily from two interrelated physiological shifts common in aging: reduced red blood cell (RBC) lifespan and increased prevalence of hemoglobin variants or chronic anemias. In healthy adults, RBCs circulate for about 120 days—giving HbA1c a stable window for glycemic estimation. But in older adults with iron-deficiency anemia, vitamin B12/folate deficiency, chronic kidney disease (CKD), or inflammation (e.g., from heart failure or rheumatoid arthritis), RBC survival drops—sometimes to just 60–90 days. Shorter lifespan = less time for hemoglobin to glycate → artificially low HbA1c, even with elevated average glucose. Conversely, some hemoglobinopathies (e.g., HbS, HbC, HbE) interfere with HbA1c assay methods—leading to falsely high or low results depending on the lab technique used. Studies show up to 15–20% of adults over 75 have at least one hemoglobin variant or undiagnosed anemia, and among those with diabetes, nearly one in four shows clinically significant HbA1c discordance fructosamine seniors.

Fructosamine, by contrast, measures glycated serum proteins (mostly albumin), which turn over every ~2–3 weeks. It therefore reflects average glucose over the prior 2–3 weeks—not 3 months—and is unaffected by RBC lifespan or most hemoglobin variants. A fructosamine level of 200–285 µmol/L generally corresponds to an estimated average glucose (eAG) of ~110–180 mg/dL—similar to an HbA1c of 5.5–7.5%. When fructosamine and HbA1c disagree by >0.5 percentage points *and* CGM-derived time-in-range (TIR) supports one over the other, that’s a strong signal to re-evaluate which metric best represents true glycemia.

## How to Accurately Assess Glycemic Control in This Population

Accurate assessment requires triangulating three data sources—not relying on any single one:

1. **HbA1c**: Still valuable—but interpret cautiously. Always check the lab method (e.g., HPLC vs. immunoassay) and review the complete blood count (CBC) for signs of anemia (low hemoglobin, MCV <80 fL, reticulocyte count >2%). If hemoglobin is <12 g/dL in women or <13 g/dL in men, or MCV is abnormal, treat HbA1c as potentially biased.

2. **Fructosamine**: Best used serially (every 2–4 weeks) to track trends—not absolute values. Albumin levels must be normal (3.5–5.0 g/dL); low albumin (common in frailty or liver/kidney disease) will lower fructosamine independently of glucose. Adjust fructosamine using the formula: *Corrected fructosamine = measured fructosamine × (4.0 / actual albumin)* — though this correction remains debated in geriatrics.

3. **CGM Metrics**: Time-in-range (TIR), glucose management indicator (GMI), and coefficient of variation (CV) add dynamic context. For adults 73+, a TIR of ≥50% in 70–180 mg/dL is a reasonable goal; GMI should align closely with fructosamine-derived eAG. If GMI is 7.8% but fructosamine suggests 6.9%, and HbA1c reads 6.2%, the discordance points toward HbA1c underestimation—likely due to shortened RBC survival.

Importantly: CGM accuracy can also be subtly affected in anemia (due to tissue hypoxia altering interstitial glucose dynamics), so sensor readings should be calibrated against fingerstick glucose—especially during acute illness or rapid hematologic change.

## Who Should Pay Special Attention to This Discordance?

Three groups of older adults warrant extra vigilance:

- **Those with confirmed or suspected hemoglobinopathies**, including sickle cell trait (present in ~8% of Black Americans), beta-thalassemia trait (common in Mediterranean, Southeast Asian, and African ancestry), or HbC disease. These conditions affect HbA1c assays differently—some methods overestimate, others underestimate.

- **Individuals with chronic anemia of inflammation or CKD**, particularly stage 3–4 (eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73m²). Up to 40% of older adults with CKD have anemia—and HbA1c can be falsely low by 0.3–1.0% compared to actual glycemia.

- **People recently hospitalized for heart failure, infection, or GI bleeding**, where acute RBC loss or stress-induced erythropoietin suppression causes transient but profound HbA1c discordance. In one study, 28% of hospitalized seniors with diabetes had HbA1c discordance fructosamine seniors that resolved within 6 weeks post-discharge.

## Practical Guidance for Daily Management

Managing diabetes well in later life means prioritizing safety, simplicity, and individualized goals. Here’s how to apply this knowledge:

- **Use fructosamine as your primary lab metric** when anemia or hemoglobinopathy is present—and repeat it every 4–6 weeks until stability is confirmed. Pair it with monthly CGM reports (if using one) focusing on TIR, hypoglycemia (<70 mg/dL), and glycemic variability (CV <36% is ideal).

- **Self-monitor with fingerstick glucose** 2–4 times weekly—not to chase perfection, but to validate patterns: e.g., if fructosamine suggests rising glucose but CGM shows good TIR, check for calibration drift or postprandial spikes missed by sensor algorithms.

- **Review medications carefully**: Metformin doses may need reduction in CKD; sulfonylureas carry higher hypoglycemia risk in frailty; GLP-1 RAs and insulin require close attention to weight, appetite, and renal function.

- **Prioritize nutrition and hydration**: Protein intake supports albumin synthesis (critical for fructosamine reliability); iron/B12/folate status should be evaluated annually in those with unexplained anemia.

Tracking your blood pressure trends can help you and your doctor make better decisions. Consider keeping a daily log or using a monitoring tool to stay informed.

- **See your doctor promptly if**:  
  • You experience recurrent hypoglycemia (especially unaware) despite “normal” HbA1c  
  • Fructosamine rises steadily while HbA1c stays flat—or vice versa  
  • You develop new fatigue, pallor, shortness of breath, or dizziness alongside changing glucose metrics  
  • Your CBC shows worsening anemia (hemoglobin drop >1.5 g/dL over 3 months)

## Conclusion

Understanding and responding to HbA1c discordance fructosamine seniors isn’t about complicating diabetes care—it’s about personalizing it. For older adults, especially those navigating anemia or hemoglobin variants, glycemic assessment is less about hitting a universal number and more about interpreting what each test *actually measures* in *your* body. With thoughtful integration of fructosamine, CGM data, and clinical context, you and your care team can make safer, more effective treatment decisions. If you're unsure, talking to your doctor is always a good idea.

### FAQ

#### Why does HbA1c not match my glucose monitor in older age?

HbA1c relies on stable red blood cell turnover over ~3 months. In adults 73+, anemia, chronic inflammation, kidney disease, or hemoglobin variants can shorten red cell lifespan or interfere with glycation—making HbA1c falsely low or high. Fructosamine, reflecting 2–3 weeks of glucose exposure via albumin, often aligns better with real-time glucose data in these cases.

#### What is HbA1c discordance fructosamine seniors—and how common is it?

HbA1c discordance fructosamine seniors refers to clinically meaningful disagreement between HbA1c and fructosamine values in older adults due to altered hemoglobin metabolism. It occurs in an estimated 15–25% of adults over 70 with diabetes and comorbid anemia or hemoglobinopathy—and is underrecognized in routine care.

#### Can fructosamine replace HbA1c for seniors with anemia?

In many cases, yes—especially when anemia is confirmed (e.g., low hemoglobin + abnormal MCV or reticulocyte count). Fructosamine is not affected by RBC lifespan or most hemoglobin variants, making it a more reliable short-term glycemic marker. However, it should be interpreted alongside CGM data and clinical symptoms—not in isolation.

#### Does low albumin affect fructosamine results?

Yes. Since fructosamine measures glycated albumin, low serum albumin (e.g., <3.5 g/dL, seen in malnutrition, liver disease, or nephrotic syndrome) will lower fructosamine—even with normal glucose. Always review albumin levels when interpreting fructosamine in older adults.

#### How often should I check fructosamine if I have anemia and diabetes?

For initial evaluation, check fructosamine every 2–4 weeks until trends stabilize. Once consistent, testing every 3–6 months is reasonable—especially if HbA1c remains discordant or if clinical status changes (e.g., new diagnosis of CKD, iron supplementation, or hospitalization).
