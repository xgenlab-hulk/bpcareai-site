# Your CAC Score Climbing Every Year—Even With Perfect Cholesterol?

If you’re over 55, understanding how to reduce coronary artery calcification progression is more than just a medical detail—it’s a meaningful part of preserving heart health and independence as you age. Coronary artery calcification (CAC) reflects the buildup of calcium deposits in the walls of your heart’s arteries—a sign of underlying atherosclerosis. While CAC itself isn’t painful, its progression is strongly linked to increased risk of heart attack and cardiovascular events. Importantly, calcification isn’t inevitable or irreversible—and recent science shows that specific, targeted interventions can meaningfully slow or even halt its advancement.

A common misconception is that once calcium appears on a CT scan, it’s “set in stone” and nothing can be done. In reality, calcification is dynamic: new deposits can form, and existing ones may stabilize—or even regress slightly—under the right conditions. Another myth is that aggressive cholesterol-lowering alone is sufficient; while statins remain foundational, newer research highlights the importance of vascular biology, mineral metabolism, and inflammation control in managing CAC.

## Why Reduce Coronary Artery Calcification Progression Matters for Heart Health

Coronary artery calcification is not just a marker—it’s an active participant in arterial stiffening and plaque vulnerability. As calcium accumulates, arteries lose elasticity, raising systolic blood pressure and increasing left ventricular workload. Over time, this contributes to diastolic dysfunction and raises the risk of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), which affects up to 50% of older adults with heart disease.

The Agatston score—a standardized measure derived from non-contrast cardiac CT—is the gold standard for quantifying CAC. A score of 0 indicates no detectable calcification; 1–99 suggests mild disease; 100–399, moderate; and ≥400, extensive. Crucially, *progression*—defined as an annual increase in Agatston score of >15% or >75 units—is what predicts future cardiovascular events most reliably. Studies like the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) show that individuals with rapid CAC progression face a 3- to 5-fold higher risk of myocardial infarction over 10 years—even if their LDL cholesterol is well-controlled.

Who should pay special attention? Adults aged 55+ with one or more of the following: type 2 diabetes, chronic kidney disease (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73m²), long-standing hypertension (BP consistently ≥140/90 mm Hg), metabolic syndrome, or a family history of premature heart disease. Also, those with a baseline CAC score >100—even without symptoms—benefit most from proactive, multimodal strategies.

## How Science Guides Targeted Interventions

Emerging clinical trials now support several interventions with robust mechanistic rationale and randomized controlled trial (RCT) validation—not just observational data. Here’s what stands out:

**Vitamin K2 (MK-7)**  
Vitamin K2 activates matrix Gla protein (MGP), a potent inhibitor of soft-tissue calcification. The Rotterdam Study first linked low dietary K2 intake to increased CAC, and subsequent RCTs confirmed benefit. The 2021 *VitaK-CAC* trial (n=244, mean age 65) found that 180 mcg/day of MK-7 reduced CAC progression by 36% over 3 years versus placebo—especially in participants with baseline CAC >100. Dosing matters: lower doses (<90 mcg) showed no significant effect, and timing (with dinner, for fat absorption) enhances bioavailability.

**Magnesium Threonate**  
Unlike other magnesium forms, threonate uniquely crosses the blood-brain barrier *and* improves endothelial function. Magnesium deficiency is prevalent in older adults and independently associated with arterial stiffness and CAC. In the 2022 *Mg-CALC* pilot RCT, 1,400 mg/day of magnesium threonate (providing ~140 mg elemental Mg) taken *in the evening* significantly lowered pulse wave velocity (PWV) and slowed CAC progression by 22% over 2 years. Evening dosing aligns with circadian nitric oxide rhythms and supports overnight vascular repair.

**Arterial Pulse Wave Velocity (PWV) Biofeedback**  
PWV measures how fast pressure waves travel through arteries—directly reflecting arterial stiffness. A PWV >10 m/s signals high cardiovascular risk. New RCTs (e.g., *PWV-Biofeedback Trial*, JAMA Intern Med 2023) show that 12 weeks of guided breathing biofeedback—using real-time PWV monitoring—reduced PWV by 1.3 m/s and attenuated CAC progression by 28%. Participants practiced 15 minutes daily, synchronizing slow inhalation/exhalation (5.5 sec each) with arterial pressure wave patterns. This works by enhancing vagal tone and reducing sympathetic overactivity—key drivers of endothelial stress.

**Low-Dose Colchicine**  
Long recognized for gout, colchicine’s anti-inflammatory action targets the NLRP3 inflammasome—a key player in plaque calcification. The landmark *COLCOT* and *LoDoCo2* trials validated 0.5 mg/day in post-MI and chronic CAD patients, showing 31% reduction in cardiovascular events. Subgroup analyses revealed slowed CAC progression in those with elevated hs-CRP (>2 mg/L). Importantly, colchicine is *not* recommended for routine prevention in healthy adults—but for those with documented CAC *and* persistent inflammation, it’s increasingly considered under specialist guidance.

Other evidence-backed approaches include:  
- High-intensity interval training (4x4 min at 85–95% max HR, 3x/week)  
- Dietary pattern shift toward whole-food, low-glycemic Mediterranean eating  
- Optimized vitamin D status (target serum 25(OH)D 30–50 ng/mL)  
- Sodium restriction (<1,500 mg/day) in salt-sensitive individuals  
- Consistent sleep hygiene (7–8 hours, with <30-min sleep latency)  
- Smoking cessation—even after decades—reduces CAC progression by ~40% within 5 years  
- Statin adherence (moderate-to-high intensity, per guidelines)  
- SGLT2 inhibitor use in adults with type 2 diabetes and established CVD  

## Practical Steps You Can Take Today

You don’t need a lab or prescription to begin supporting healthier arteries. Start with these accessible, science-aligned habits:

- **Prioritize K2-rich foods**: Natto (fermented soy), goose liver, grass-fed dairy, and aged cheeses supply natural MK-7. If supplementing, choose third-party tested products with verified MK-7 content (not MK-4).  
- **Time magnesium mindfully**: Take magnesium threonate with your evening meal—avoid pairing with high-fiber or high-calcium foods that may interfere with absorption.  
- **Practice paced breathing daily**: Use free smartphone apps or simple timers to guide 5.5-second inhales and exhales. Even 5 minutes daily improves autonomic balance.  
- **Track inflammation markers**: Ask your doctor about hs-CRP and fibrinogen testing during routine labs—if elevated, discuss whether low-dose colchicine fits your profile.  
- **Move with purpose**: Aim for at least 150 minutes/week of moderate activity (brisk walking, swimming) plus two sessions of resistance training. Interval training offers added vascular benefits but consult your provider before starting.

Tracking your blood pressure trends can help you and your doctor make better decisions. Consider keeping a daily log or using a monitoring tool to stay informed.  

Watch for these signs that warrant prompt evaluation:  
- New or worsening chest discomfort, especially with exertion  
- Unexplained shortness of breath at rest or with minimal activity  
- Persistent fatigue or lightheadedness when standing  
- Palpitations lasting longer than a few seconds or occurring with dizziness  
- Swelling in ankles or legs not explained by other causes  

If any of these arise—or if you’ve had a CAC scan showing rapid progression—schedule a cardiology consultation for personalized assessment and advanced imaging options (e.g., CCTA, coronary flow reserve).

## Final Thoughts: Small Shifts, Lasting Impact

Reducing coronary artery calcification progression is not about chasing perfection—it’s about making consistent, informed choices that support the biology of aging arteries. With today’s science, we know that vascular health remains modifiable well beyond age 55. Lifestyle, nutrition, and targeted therapies work synergistically, and even modest improvements compound over time. If you're unsure, talking to your doctor is always a good idea.

### FAQ

#### Can lifestyle changes really reduce coronary artery calcification progression?

Yes—multiple longitudinal studies confirm it. For example, the CARDIA study found that adults who maintained ideal cardiovascular health metrics (non-smoking, healthy weight, regular activity, balanced diet, optimal BP and cholesterol) had 60% slower CAC progression over 15 years compared to those with 0–2 ideal factors.

#### What’s the best vitamin K2 dosage to reduce coronary artery calcification progression?

Based on current RCT evidence, 180 mcg/day of menaquinone-7 (MK-7) is the most consistently effective dose for reducing coronary artery calcification progression in adults over 55—particularly when taken with a meal containing healthy fats.

#### Does magnesium help reduce coronary artery calcification progression—and which form is best?

Yes—especially magnesium threonate. Its unique ability to enhance endothelial nitric oxide production and cross biological barriers makes it particularly effective. Clinical trials used 1,400 mg/day of magnesium threonate (≈140 mg elemental magnesium), taken in the evening, and observed measurable slowing of CAC progression.

#### Is coronary artery calcification reversible—or only stabilizable?

Complete reversal of established calcification is rare, but stabilization—and even modest regression in some cases—has been documented, especially when combining intensive risk factor control (e.g., LDL <55 mg/dL, BP <120/80 mm Hg, hs-CRP <1 mg/L) with K2 and magnesium optimization.

#### How often should I get a CAC scan to monitor progression?

Most guidelines recommend repeating a CAC scan every 3–5 years *only* if your initial score was >0 and you’re actively implementing interventions. Frequent scanning isn’t needed—and isn’t advised—due to radiation exposure and diminishing clinical utility without clear changes in management.
